What steady state misses

Two different questions

Steady state

What does the aquifer settle down to if this pumping never changes?

Useful. Not what anyone asks.

Transient

When does the water table reach the pump intake?

How much of what we pump this year is coming out of storage?

The second set needs time in the model — and storage properties, which a steady-state run does not even require you to supply.

Stress periods and time steps

  • A stress period is an interval over which every stress is constant. Change the pumping rate, you need a new period.
  • A time step is a solver interval within a period. More steps means a better transient response and a slower run.
perlen = [1.0, 30.0] + [365.0] * 10     # length of each period
nstp   = [1,   30]   + [4] * 10         # steps within each
steady = [True] + [False] * 11          # one flag per period

Your turn: read the discretization

Hint

The transient days are the perlen values whose steady flag is False. zip pairs them up.

Solution

n_periods = len(perlen)
n_steps = sum(nstp)
simulated_days = sum(p for p, s in zip(perlen, steady) if not s)

12 periods, 71 steps, 3,680 days — just over ten years.

Note the odd 30-day period with daily steps. The interesting part of a transient response happens at the start, and a quarterly time step walks straight over it. Fine early steps cost almost nothing.

Resolve what you came to see

Choosing time steps is the same problem as choosing cell size, and it has the same answer: finer than the feature you are trying to observe.

A well switching on produces most of its head change in days. Sample it quarterly and your hydrograph starts at the answer.

Storage

Two properties, three orders of magnitude apart

Property Symbol Applies when Typical Units
Specific yield sy unconfined — pores drain 0.05 – 0.30 —
Specific storage ss confined — water expands, matrix compresses 1e-6 – 1e-4 1/m

A confined aquifer releases almost no water per metre of decline. That is why confined drawdown propagates so far, so fast.

Your turn: how much water is in there?

Hint

Specific yield is dimensionless — multiply by area alone. Specific storage is per metre of aquifer, so it needs the thickness too.

Solution

unconfined = sy * cell_area
confined = ss * cell_area * thickness

9,375 m³ against 125 m³ — a factor of 75.

The ratio is sy / (ss × b), so it depends on thickness: a thin confined aquifer is even more extreme. What it means is that the same well drains an unconfined aquifer over years and a confined one over days, before either has to start capturing flow from elsewhere.

An upper bound worth computing

Across all 2,400 cells, 1 m of decline in our confined aquifer releases 300,000 m³.

At 20,000 m³/d, that is 15 days of pumping.

And that is an upper bound, because nothing like the whole aquifer drops a metre — drawdown is concentrated near the well.

So a confined aquifer reaches something close to steady state quickly. The Week 11 steady-state model was answering a question about the near future, which is worth knowing before you dismiss it.

The steady-state warm-up

Why period 0 has no pumping

A transient model needs an initial head field. Any field you invent is out of equilibrium with the boundaries.

  • So the model spends its first years relaxing toward balance
  • And you cannot tell that artificial drift from the response you came to measure

Run one steady-state period first, with no pumping. Its heads are already in equilibrium. Everything after that is the response to the stress you added.

Note that the dictionary has an entry for every period. If you skip one, FloPy silently reuses the previous period’s data — which is convenient when you mean it and a very quiet bug when you don’t.

Wrap up

What you did today

  • Steady state answers “what settles down to”; transient answers “when”
  • Stress periods hold stresses constant; time steps subdivide them
  • sy and ss differ by a factor of sy / (ss·b) — about 75 here
  • 15 days of storage in a confined aquifer, as an upper bound
  • Start every transient run with a no-pumping steady-state period
