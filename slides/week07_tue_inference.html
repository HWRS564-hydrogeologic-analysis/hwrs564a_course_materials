One question, all period

Did typical depth to water change at Tucson-area monitoring wells after a major shift in the city’s water-supply operations?

Observed change → changes expected under no effect → is ours unusual?

By the end, you should be able to identify the independent unit, report a paired change in feet, build a sign-flip null distribution, and explain what a p-value and bootstrap interval do—and do not—say.

Start with the groundwater

A physical event fixes the comparison

Around 2000–01, Tucson Water shifted pumping toward Avra Valley recharge-and-recovery facilities and began delivering a blend containing recharged Colorado River water.

Sources: Arizona Department of Water Resources and Tucson Water.

Among Tucson-area wells monitored in both windows, how did median depth to water change from 1996–2000 to 2002–2006?

  • 2001 is treated as a transition year.
  • A well needs observations in at least three years of each window.
  • Each well contributes one pre median and one post median.
  • Positive change means water became deeper below land surface.

The event date came from operations records, not from searching the hydrograph. This before/after comparison does not by itself tell us why levels changed.

What counts as one observation?

The source file contains 866 tape readings from the selected wells.

Not independent

519 pre-period readings and 347 post-period readings.

Independent units for this analysis

18 paired monitoring wells.

Readings from one well share location, construction, screened interval, and aquifer history. Different CSV rows do not automatically become replicates.

Let every well be its own control

Depths range from about 20 to 330 ft across wells. Pairing separates those large site-to-site differences from the period comparison.

For well \(i\),

\[d_i=\text{post median}_i-\text{pre median}_i.\]

The analysis uses the 18 changes \(d_i\). Our target quantity is their mean.

Calculate the observed effect

Hint

Use the array’s .mean() method.

Solution

mean_change = change_ft.mean()

The observed mean change is +12.47 ft. Positive means the water table was deeper in the later window.

See the observations behind the mean

Most wells became deeper, but not all. The mean preserves that variation.

Imagine no systematic period effect

The null hypothesis in ordinary language

Null: across these kinds of wells, there is no systematic pre/post change.

Written compactly:

\[H_0:\text{mean paired change}=0\ \text{ft}.\]

If the null were a reasonable description, “pre” and “post” could be swapped within each well without systematically changing the result.

A label swap is a sign flip

For one well:

\[d_i=\text{post}-\text{pre}.\]

Swapping its labels gives

\[\text{pre}-\text{post}=-d_i.\]

Under the null, randomly keep or reverse each of the 18 signs, then calculate the mean again.

Keep the well pairs intact. Do not shuffle individual tape readings.

Try one null world

Hint

Multiply every change by its sign, then take the mean.

Solution

null_once = (change_ft * signs).mean()

One shuffled result is not enough. Repeat the same operation many times to see the full range expected under the null.

Build the null distribution

Read the p-value as a conditional statement

Here \(p\approx0.001\).

If there were no systematic period effect and within-well labels were exchangeable, only about 1 in 1,000 random sign assignments would produce a mean change at least this far from zero.

It is not a 0.1% probability that the null is true.

  • Size: +12.47 ft gives magnitude and direction.
  • Evidence: \(p\approx0.001\) says the result is unusual under the null.
  • Cause: neither number isolates pumping, recharge, climate, or aquifer lag.

How uncertain is +12.47 ft?

Permutation and bootstrap have different jobs

Procedure What is resampled? Question
permutation pre/post labels within wells Would this be unusual under no effect?
bootstrap wells, with replacement How much might the estimated mean vary?

Both resample wells, because wells are the observational units.

Bootstrap the mean change

Hint

Store boot_sample.mean(), then use np.percentile(boot_means, [2.5, 97.5]).

Solution

boot_means[i] = boot_sample.mean()
ci_low, ci_high = np.percentile(boot_means, [2.5, 97.5])

The interval is approximately [6.58, 18.39] ft.

What that interval leaves out

The bootstrap describes variability from observing a different collection of wells similar to these 18.

It does not repair:

  • nonrandom monitoring locations;
  • screened-interval differences;
  • datum or measurement errors; or
  • the lack of an untreated comparison group.

Finish with a bounded conclusion

Four sentences

  1. Result: what changed, by how much, and in what unit?
  2. Uncertainty: what interval and p-value accompany it?
  3. Assumption: what was treated as independent or exchangeable?
  4. Limitation: what prevents a broader or causal conclusion?

Across 18 paired Tucson-area monitoring wells, median depth to water was on average 12.47 ft greater in 2002–2006 than in 1996–2000 (95% bootstrap interval: 6.58 to 18.39 ft; paired permutation \(p\approx0.001\)).

The analysis treats wells—not individual tape readings—as independent and assumes pre/post labels are exchangeable within wells under the null. Because the monitored wells were not randomly sampled and there is no untreated control, the result does not describe the entire aquifer or establish a cause.
