Filtering

A mask is a Series of booleans

Exactly last week’s numpy mask, with labels along for the ride.

Combining conditions

deep_and_high = wells[
    (wells["depth_ft"] > 500) & (wells["elev_ft"] > 2680)
]
  • & for and, | for or, ~ for not
  • Every condition needs its own parentheses
  • and / or do not work here — they ask for one truth value, and a Series has many

Drop the parentheses and & binds tighter than >, so Python tries 500 & wells["elev_ft"] and the error names neither of the things you wrote.

The silent one

A comparison against NaN is always False, so missing rows are dropped and never mentioned. In the real inventory that is 184 wells with no depth on file. Check .isna().sum() before you filter, not after you’re confused.

Your turn: find the shallow northwest wells

Latitude above 32.3, longitude below −111.0, depth under 200 ft.

Hint 1

Three conditions joined by &, each wrapped in its own parentheses.

Hint 2

Longitudes here are negative, so “west of −111.0” means less than −111.0.

Solution

nw_shallow = wells[
    (wells["latitude"] > 32.3)
    & (wells["longitude"] < -111.0)
    & (wells["depth_ft"] < 200)
]

Wells A and C. Well D is far enough north but too far east; E is west enough but too far south. Negative longitudes reverse the direction your intuition expects, so say the comparison out loud before you trust it.

New columns

Assign to a name that doesn’t exist

wells["depth_m"] = wells["depth_ft"] * 0.3048
wells["screen_bottom_elev"] = wells["elev_ft"] - wells["depth_ft"]

Elementwise, like numpy. Nothing new — which is the point.

The warning you will see:

SettingWithCopyWarning: A value is trying to be set on a copy of a slice

Take an explicit .copy() when you slice a subset you plan to modify. That is the fix, every time.

Your turn: decline since the first reading

depth_to_water_ft counts downward, so a rising number means a falling water table. Compute how far the level has dropped since the record began.

Hint

Subtract the first value of the column from the whole column. .iloc[0] gets the first element regardless of what the index labels are.

Solution

one_site["decline_ft"] = (
    one_site["depth_to_water_ft"] - one_site["depth_to_water_ft"].iloc[0]
)

111 ft of decline in one well in one lifetime.

These six numbers are made up for the slide. The real well behind them — D-15-13 11CBA — declined by that much between 1931 and 1994 without turning around; whether any Tucson well has recovered is a question the Week 8 lab makes you answer from the data rather than take from me.

Plotting

Enough to see what you have

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 4))
ax.plot(one_site["date"], one_site["depth_to_water_ft"])
ax.invert_yaxis()
ax.set_ylabel("depth to water (ft below land surface)")

This is the plot you make while you’re working, not the one you show people. Week 8 is about the second kind.

invert_yaxis() is not cosmetic

Without it, depth increases upward, so a falling water table draws as a rising line.

Someone will read that as recovery. In a committee meeting. About your work.

When the quantity counts downward, so should the axis. This costs one line and prevents a misreading you cannot take back.

Your turn: fix the axis

This plots a falling water table as a rising line. Add the one call that fixes it, and label the axis so nobody has to guess which way is down.

Hint

The method flips the y axis so larger values sit lower. The label needs to say both the quantity and which direction it counts.

Solution

ax.invert_yaxis()
ax.set_ylabel("depth to water (ft below land surface)")

"depth (ft)" alone would not be enough. Below land surface, or above a datum? Those differ by about 2,600 ft here, and only the label says which you meant.
