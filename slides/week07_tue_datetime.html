Dates as dates

Strings are not dates

df = pd.read_csv("flow.csv")
df["datetime"].dtype        # object  -> these are strings

Sorting gives you alphabetical order. "2015-1-5" and "2015-01-05" are different values. You cannot subtract them.

df = pd.read_csv("flow.csv", parse_dates=["datetime"])
df = df.set_index("datetime").sort_index()

Now you have a DatetimeIndex, and an entire API opens up.

Partial-string selection

The most useful thing in this deck.

Note the slice is inclusive of both ends — the same .loc rule from Week 5, and here it is exactly what you want.

.dt for a column that isn’t the index

levels["date"].dt.year
levels["date"].dt.month
levels["date"].dt.dayofyear
levels["date"].dt.quarter

Use the index API when the dates are the index, and .dt when they are a column you’re grouping by. Long-format data — one row per measurement, well in a column — usually wants .dt.

Your turn: select the monsoon

Pull July through September of 2006 out of a daily series.

Hint

A slice of two partial strings. You don’t need to know that September has 30 days — pandas works out the end of the month for you.

Solution

monsoon = q.loc["2006-07":"2006-09"]

92 days. "2006-09" as the end of a slice means the end of September, not the first of September — which is the inclusive rule doing something genuinely convenient for once.

Resampling

groupby for time

q.resample("ME").mean()      # month end
q.resample("YE").sum()       # year end
q.resample("W").max()        # weekly

7,305 daily values become 240 monthly or 20 annual ones.

Frequency strings changed in pandas 2.2. It is ME and YE now, not M and Y. Older tutorials use the old ones and you’ll get a FutureWarning — another instance of last week’s theme.

The aggregation is a hydrology decision

You want Use
average conditions .mean()
total volume .sum()
flood risk .max()
a typical day, ignoring floods .median()

Summing cfs over a month gives you cfs-days, which is a volume and a perfectly good one — as long as the axis label says so.

Sabino Creek: the wettest year averaged 24.6 cfs, the driest 2.6. A factor of ten. Any analysis built on “the average year” describes a year that does not occur.

The water year

Hydrologists count years from October 1 to September 30.

  • A winter snowpack and the melt it feeds belong to the same year
  • A calendar year splits them down the middle
  • October 1 is the driest, least eventful moment in most of the western US
q.resample("YE-SEP").sum()      # years ending 30 September

Water year 2007 runs from 1 October 2006 to 30 September 2007. It is named for the year it ends in. Getting this wrong shifts your entire record by three months.

Your turn: annual totals, two ways

Hint

Year-end is "YE". To end the year in a different month, append the month’s three-letter abbreviation with a hyphen.

Solution

calendar = q.resample("YE").sum()
water_year = q.resample("YE-SEP").sum()

20 calendar years and 21 water years, from the same data. The extra one is a stub: the record starts on 1 January 2005, which is already three months into water year 2005, so that first bin is incomplete.

Watch for that stub. It will be the lowest bar on your chart, and it is an artifact of where the record starts.

Your turn: which water year?

A storm on 12 November 2013. Which water year does it belong to, and how would you compute that from a date?

Hint

October, November, and December belong to the next water year. Everything else belongs to its own calendar year. A comparison returns True, and True is 1.

Solution

water_year = d.year + (d.month >= 10)

Water year 2014. November 2013 is already two months into it.

That one-liner works on a whole column too: df["date"].dt.year + (df["date"].dt.month >= 10). Adding a boolean to an integer is idiomatic here, not a trick — True is 1 and it reads cleanly.

Resample vs. groupby

resample keeps time moving forward. groupby on a month number collapses across years. The second one is a climatology — the long-term average January — and it is a different object entirely.

What you did today
