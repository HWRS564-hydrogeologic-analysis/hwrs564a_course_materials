Ordinary least squares, one step at a time

HWRS 564a · Week 9 · Tuesday, October 20

Dr. Andrew Bennett

Today

The job for this class

Starting only with a line and a definition of error, derive

\[ \hat\beta_1= \frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)} {\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}, \qquad \hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x. \]

Then connect those coefficients to residuals, \(R^2\), standard error, and a confidence interval. No formula will arrive without its reason.

Define the problem

A line is a model for the mean

For observation \(i\),

\[Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i+\varepsilon_i.\]

  • \(Y_i\) is a random response; \(y_i\) is the value we observed.
  • \(x_i\) is the predictor: elapsed time in years.
  • \(\beta_0+\beta_1x_i\) is the mean response at \(x_i\).
  • \(\varepsilon_i\) is the departure from that mean.

We do not know \(\beta_0\) or \(\beta_1\). We choose estimates \(b_0\) and \(b_1\) from the observations.

A candidate line creates one residual per point

The prediction at \(x_i\) is

\[\hat y_i=b_0+b_1x_i.\]

The observed vertical error is

\[e_i=y_i-\hat y_i=y_i-b_0-b_1x_i.\]

Positive and negative residuals cancel if we simply add them. Absolute values would work numerically, but their corner at zero prevents this simple algebra. Least squares uses squared residuals.

The objective is a function of two unknowns

\[ S(b_0,b_1) =\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_i^2 =\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-b_0-b_1x_i)^2. \]

  • The data \(x_i,y_i\) are fixed numbers in this minimisation.
  • The intercept \(b_0\) and slope \(b_1\) are variables.
  • The best-fitting line is the pair where \(S\) is smallest.

We need a minimum in two directions, so both partial derivatives must equal zero.

Solve for the intercept

Differentiate with respect to \(b_0\)

Start with

\[S=\sum_i(y_i-b_0-b_1x_i)^2.\]

Use the chain rule on each squared term:

\[ \frac{\partial S}{\partial b_0} =\sum_i2(y_i-b_0-b_1x_i)(-1). \]

Therefore

\[\frac{\partial S}{\partial b_0}=-2\sum_i(y_i-b_0-b_1x_i).\]

Set the derivative equal to zero

\[-2\sum_i(y_i-b_0-b_1x_i)=0\]

Divide by \(-2\) and distribute the sum:

\[\sum_i y_i-\sum_i b_0-b_1\sum_i x_i=0.\]

Because \(b_0\) is constant across observations,

\[\sum_i b_0=nb_0.\]

So

\[\sum_i y_i-nb_0-b_1\sum_i x_i=0.\]

Divide by \(n\) and recognise the means

Rearrange the previous equation:

\[nb_0=\sum_i y_i-b_1\sum_i x_i.\]

Divide every term by \(n\):

\[ b_0=\frac{1}{n}\sum_i y_i -b_1\frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_i. \]

Since \(\bar y=n^{-1}\sum_i y_i\) and \(\bar x=n^{-1}\sum_i x_i\),

\[\boxed{b_0=\bar y-b_1\bar x}.\]

Every least-squares line with an intercept passes through \((\bar x,\bar y)\).

Your turn: use the first normal equation

Hint

Use \(b_0=\bar y-b_1\bar x\).

Solution

x_bar = x.mean()
y_bar = y.mean()
b0 = y_bar - b1 * x_bar

\(\bar x=1.5\), \(\bar y=14\), and \(b_0=9.8\).

Solve for the slope

Differentiate with respect to \(b_1\)

Again start with

\[S=\sum_i(y_i-b_0-b_1x_i)^2.\]

Now the inner derivative is \(-x_i\):

\[ \frac{\partial S}{\partial b_1} =\sum_i2(y_i-b_0-b_1x_i)(-x_i). \]

Set it equal to zero and divide by \(-2\):

\[\sum_i x_i(y_i-b_0-b_1x_i)=0.\]

Substitute the intercept relationship

Use \(b_0=\bar y-b_1\bar x\):

\[ \sum_i x_i[y_i-(\bar y-b_1\bar x)-b_1x_i]=0. \]

Remove the parentheses:

\[ \sum_i x_i(y_i-\bar y+b_1\bar x-b_1x_i)=0. \]

Factor \(b_1\) inside the final two terms:

\[ \sum_i x_i[(y_i-\bar y)-b_1(x_i-\bar x)]=0. \]

Centring removes the distracting cross-terms

Define centred values

\[X_i=x_i-\bar x,\qquad Y_i=y_i-\bar y.\]

Because \(\sum_iX_i=0\) and \(\sum_iY_i=0\), the second normal equation can be written as

\[\sum_iX_i(Y_i-b_1X_i)=0.\]

Distribute:

\[\sum_iX_iY_i-b_1\sum_iX_i^2=0.\]

Move the second term:

\[b_1\sum_iX_i^2=\sum_iX_iY_i.\]

Divide to obtain the slope

If the \(x_i\) are not all identical, then \(\sum_iX_i^2>0\). Divide both sides:

\[ \boxed{ b_1=\frac{\sum_iX_iY_i}{\sum_iX_i^2} =\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)} {\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2} }. \]

Numerator: how \(x\) and \(y\) vary together. Denominator: how much \(x\) varies. If \(x\) has no variation, a slope cannot be estimated.

Covariance divided by variance

Sample covariance and variance use the same factor \(1/(n-1)\):

\[ s_{xy}=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{n-1}, \qquad s_x^2=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}{n-1}. \]

Their ratio cancels that factor:

\[\boxed{b_1=\frac{s_{xy}}{s_x^2}}.\]

The sign comes from covariance. The magnitude also depends on the units and spread of \(x\).

Your turn: build the slope from centred sums

Hint

Compute the centred arrays first. Multiply X * Y elementwise before summing.

Solution

X = x - x_bar
Y = y - y_bar
sxy_sum = (X * Y).sum()
sxx_sum = (X ** 2).sum()
b1 = sxy_sum / sxx_sum
b0 = y_bar - b1 * x_bar

\(\sum X_iY_i=14\), \(\sum X_i^2=5\), so \(\hat y=9.8+2.8x\).

Check what the solution guarantees

The fitted residuals satisfy both normal equations

At the minimum,

\[\sum_i e_i=0,\qquad \sum_i x_ie_i=0.\]

The first equation says residuals balance above and below the fitted line. The second says no linear association with \(x\) remains.

These are algebraic consequences of fitting an intercept and slope. They do not prove that residuals are independent, normally distributed, or patternless.

Your turn: verify the normal equations

Hint

Prediction is b0 + b1 * x; residual is measured minus predicted.

Solution

y_hat = b0 + b1 * x
residual = y - y_hat
sum_residual = residual.sum()
sum_x_residual = (x * residual).sum()

Both sums are zero apart from floating-point roundoff.

From a line to statistical evidence

\(R^2\) compares the line with a mean-only model

Total squared variation:

\[\operatorname{TSS}=\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2.\]

Unexplained squared variation:

\[\operatorname{RSS}=\sum_i(y_i-\hat y_i)^2=\sum_i e_i^2.\]

Therefore

\[\boxed{R^2=1-\frac{\operatorname{RSS}}{\operatorname{TSS}}}.\]

\(R^2\) is a relative in-sample comparison. It is not percent accuracy and does not test the regression assumptions.

Estimate the residual variance

If the errors have common variance \(\sigma^2\), estimate it from the residuals:

\[ s^2=\frac{\operatorname{RSS}}{n-2}. \]

Why \(n-2\)?

  • We began with \(n\) observed response values.
  • Estimating \(b_0\) imposes \(\sum e_i=0\).
  • Estimating \(b_1\) imposes \(\sum x_ie_i=0\).
  • Two fitted constraints leave \(n-2\) residual degrees of freedom.

Propagate residual scatter into slope uncertainty

Under independent errors with constant variance,

\[ \operatorname{Var}(b_1)=\frac{\sigma^2}{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}. \]

Replace unknown \(\sigma\) with \(s\):

\[ \boxed{\operatorname{SE}(b_1)= \frac{s}{\sqrt{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}}}. \]

  • More residual scatter makes the slope less certain.
  • A wider span of \(x\) makes the slope more certain.
  • More rows help only to the extent that they add independent information.

Confidence interval and slope test use the same ratio

For the null hypothesis \(H_0:\beta_1=\beta_{1,0}\),

\[t=\frac{b_1-\beta_{1,0}}{\operatorname{SE}(b_1)},\qquad df=n-2.\]

A two-sided 95% confidence interval is

\[ b_1\pm t_{0.975,n-2}\operatorname{SE}(b_1). \]

The p-value asks how unusual the observed \(t\) would be if the null model and its assumptions were true. The confidence interval lists slope values not rejected by the corresponding two-sided tests.

Your turn: finish the inferential calculation

Hint

Use \(s^2=\mathrm{RSS}/(n-2)\), then \(\mathrm{SE}(b_1)=\sqrt{s^2/S_{xx}}\).

Solution

residual_variance = rss / (n - 2)
slope_se = np.sqrt(residual_variance / sxx)
margin = t_critical * slope_se
ci = (b1 - margin, b1 + margin)

The ordinary standard error is 0.036 ft/yr and the 95% interval is 2.010–2.152 ft/yr.

The interval inherits the model assumptions

Mean model

  • The relationship is adequately linear
  • Errors have mean zero at each \(x\)
  • The predictor is measured adequately

Ordinary uncertainty

  • Residuals are independent
  • Residual variance is constant
  • Normal errors give exact small-sample \(t\) inference

The line can always be calculated when \(x\) varies. The standard error, confidence interval, and p-value are trustworthy only when their assumptions are defensible. Thursday’s lab is about checking that difference.

For two independent groups, code \(x=0\) for “pre” and \(x=1\) for “post”:

\[y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i+\varepsilon_i.\]

Then

\[\hat\beta_0=\bar y_{\text{pre}},\qquad \hat\beta_1=\bar y_{\text{post}}-\bar y_{\text{pre}}.\]

Week 7 preserved pairing by first calculating \(d_i=y_{i,\text{post}}- y_{i,\text{pre}}\). The model \(d_i=\beta_0+\varepsilon_i\) estimates \(\hat\beta_0=\bar d\): the same 12.47-ft mean paired change.

A t-test and a regression are often different views of the same comparison. The design still decides which rows are independent.

Wrap up

The whole derivation

  1. Define \(e_i=y_i-b_0-b_1x_i\) and minimise \(\sum e_i^2\).
  2. \(\partial S/\partial b_0=0\) gives \(b_0=\bar y-b_1\bar x\).
  3. \(\partial S/\partial b_1=0\) gives \(b_1=\sum(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)/\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2\).
  4. Residual sums produce \(R^2\), \(s^2\), and the slope standard error.
  5. Inference requires assumptions that the algebra alone cannot verify.
