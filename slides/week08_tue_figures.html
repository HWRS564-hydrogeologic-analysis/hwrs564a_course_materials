Figure and axes

Two objects

fig — the page.

Size, resolution, saving.

ax — one set of axes on it.

Lines, labels, limits.

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4))
ax.plot(x, y)
ax.set_ylabel("head (m)")
fig.savefig("out.png", dpi=300)

Almost everything you want is a method on one or the other. Knowing which is half of learning matplotlib.

plt.plot draws somewhere

plt.plot(x, y)        # ...on whatever axes matplotlib touched last
ax.plot(x, y)         # ...here

With one plot per cell, they behave identically. With two figures, a loop, or a function that makes a plot, the plt. version starts drawing on the wrong axes and the bug is invisible in the code.

Always fig, ax = plt.subplots() first, then ax.something(). It is one extra line and it removes an entire category of confusion.

Panels

subplots(2, 2) returns a 2 × 2 array. axes.flat iterates it in reading order, which is usually what you want in a loop.

Sharing an axis is a claim

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, sharey=True)
  • sharey=True puts every panel on the same scale, so the panels are comparable
  • sharey=False lets each panel fill its own range

Three wells with 6 ft, 110 ft, and 30 ft of decline look identical on independent axes. Sharing the axis is what makes the comparison honest.

And don’t share when the quantities aren’t comparable — that makes one panel look flat for no reason.

Your turn: two stacked panels

Discharge on top, its 7-day mean below, sharing the x axis so they line up.

Hint

Sharing takes a boolean. The label needs the quantity and its unit.

Solution

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 1, figsize=(8, 4), sharex=True)
axes[0].set_ylabel("daily discharge (cfs)")

With sharex=True the top panel’s tick labels disappear automatically, which is what you want — one set of dates for both panels, and more room for the data.

Colour

Three kinds, three jobs

Data Colormap
runs one way, low to high sequential — viridis, Blues
has a meaningful middle diverging — RdBu, coolwarm
unordered categories qualitative — tab10

Using a diverging map on sequential data puts a false boundary in the middle of your range. Using a sequential map on diverging data hides the zero crossing.

Don’t use jet

jet has bright bands at cyan and yellow and dark ends, so equal steps in the data are not equal steps in apparent brightness. It invents boundaries that aren’t there and hides ones that are.

Centre a diverging map on zero

limit = change["ft"].abs().max()
ax.scatter(..., cmap="RdBu_r", vmin=-limit, vmax=limit)

Leave vmin/vmax off and matplotlib centres on the midpoint of your data. With changes from −6 to +111 ft, the neutral colour lands at +52 — so wells that dropped fifty feet render as “unchanged”.

A diverging colormap that isn’t centred on zero says the opposite of what you mean, in a way that looks completely normal.

Your turn: centre the colormap

These changes run from −6 to +111 ft. Make zero the neutral colour.

Hint

You want the limits symmetric about zero, so both come from the largest absolute value in the data.

Solution

limit = np.abs(change).max()
sc = ax.scatter(..., vmin=-limit, vmax=limit, ...)

Now white means zero and the seven recovering wells are visibly on the other side of it. Without the limits, matplotlib centres on the data midpoint of about +52 ft — so a well that dropped fifty feet renders white and reads as unchanged.

The cost is that most of the blue half of the colorbar goes unused. That is fine: an unused range is honest, a mislabelled midpoint is not.

Colour cannot be the only signal

  • About one reader in twelve has a colour vision deficiency
  • Deuteranopia makes red and green nearly identical
  • Projectors and photocopiers do their own damage

Encode every distinction at least twice.

ax.plot(x, a, color="#AB0520", ls="-",  marker="o", label="pumped")
ax.plot(x, b, color="#0C234B", ls="--", marker="s", label="control")

Two extra keyword arguments. The figure now works in greyscale, on a bad projector, and for everyone in the room.

Your turn: make it readable

This uses red and green, and nothing else, to tell two series apart.

Hint

The first line needs a marker; the second needs a dash pattern that differs from the first. "--", "-.", and ":" are all available.

Solution

ax.plot(years, a, color="#AB0520", ls="-",  marker="o", label="heavily pumped")
ax.plot(years, b, color="#0C234B", ls="--", marker="s", label="lightly pumped")

Note the colours also changed — UA red and blue differ in lightness, not just hue, so they survive a greyscale print. Red and green are nearly the same lightness, which is why that pair fails twice over.

Wrap up

What you did today

  • fig, ax = plt.subplots() first, always
  • Sharing an axis is a claim that the panels are comparable
  • Sequential, diverging, qualitative — and never jet
  • Centre a diverging map on zero, or it lies
  • Encode every distinction twice
